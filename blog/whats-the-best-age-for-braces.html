What's the Best Age for Braces? A Parent's Guide to Early Orthodontic Treatment

Dante Gonzales Orthodontics August 19, 2026 9 min read

On this page

If your child has a gap between their front teeth, a bite that doesn't quite line up, or you've simply noticed crowded teeth coming in, you have probably asked yourself the same question every parent eventually asks: what is the best age for braces? The honest answer is that there is no single exact age that applies to every child. The best age for braces depends more on your child's dental development, jaw growth, and specific orthodontic issues than on chronological age alone. At Dante Gonzales Orthodontics, we help parents understand when early orthodontic treatment makes sense, when it's better to wait, and what to expect at every stage of their child's growth.

Why Age Isn't the Only Factor

Many parents assume there's a fixed age to get braces, but orthodontists actually look at a combination of factors: how many permanent teeth have erupted, how the jaw is developing, whether baby teeth are still present, and whether any functional issues like difficulty chewing or mouth breathing are affecting the child's mouth. Two children of the same age can be at very different stages of dental development, which is why a personalized orthodontic evaluation matters more than a birthday.

That said, there are well-established age ranges that most orthodontists use as a general guide.

The First Orthodontic Evaluation: Age 7

The American Association of Orthodontists recommends that every child have their first orthodontic evaluation by age 7, even if nothing looks obviously wrong. By this age, enough permanent teeth have usually come in for an orthodontist to spot early orthodontic problems like significant crowding, protruding front teeth, narrow jaws, or bite issues while the jaw is still growing and more responsive to treatment.

Most children lose their baby teeth by ages 6 or 7, and this transitional period — when baby teeth and adult teeth are mixed in the mouth — gives an orthodontist a clear picture of how the child's jaw and teeth are likely to develop. Importantly, many children are simply monitored after this age 7 evaluation. An evaluation does not automatically mean your child needs braces right away; it simply means an orthodontist is watching your child's growth and can catch problems early, before they become harder and more expensive to correct.

Phase One (Early) Treatment: Ages 7–10

For children with significant dental issues, an orthodontist may recommend Phase One treatment, sometimes called early orthodontic treatment, typically between ages 7 and 10. This phase does not usually involve a full set of traditional braces. Instead, it may use partial braces, expanders, or other appliances designed to guide jaw growth, create room for crowded teeth, or correct habits like mouth breathing before they affect how permanent teeth come in.

Early treatment can help with:

  • Correcting jaw growth so the upper and lower jaws develop in proper position relative to each other
  • Creating space for crowded permanent teeth still waiting to erupt
  • Addressing protruding front teeth that are at higher risk of injury
  • Reducing the need for tooth extractions later in adolescence
  • Correcting oral habits, such as prolonged thumb sucking or mouth breathing, that can affect jaw shape over time

Not every child needs Phase One treatment. Some orthodontic issues are easier to correct when detected early, but many children can wait until more permanent teeth have erupted before starting any active treatment.

Phase Two: Comprehensive Treatment with Full Braces

For most children, active treatment with full braces begins between ages 9 and 14, once most permanent teeth have erupted. Most orthodontists consider ages 10 to 14 the optimal age range for traditional braces, since by this point the child has enough adult teeth in place for an orthodontist to move them into their correct, final position.

Comprehensive treatment usually begins once most permanent teeth have erupted, since starting full braces treatment before then can mean incoming adult teeth continue shifting the bite after treatment is already underway. This is also, coincidentally, around the age when children prefer to get braces alongside their peers — often between ages 11 and 13 — which can make the emotional and social adjustment easier.

Signs Your Child May Need Early Orthodontic Treatment

While every child's development is different, certain signs suggest it may be time for an orthodontic exam sooner rather than later:

  • Crowded teeth that overlap or twist as they come in, which can lead to misalignment and chewing issues
  • Gaps between teeth that are wider than expected, which can increase the risk of gum disease
  • Protruding front teeth that stick out noticeably and are at greater risk of injury
  • An underbite or crossbite, which can cause uneven wear on front teeth and difficulty chewing
  • Mouth breathing, which can affect jaw shape and tooth alignment over time
  • Difficulty chewing or biting, suggesting the upper and lower teeth aren't meeting properly
  • Losing baby teeth very early or very late compared to other children the same age
  • Thumb sucking or pacifier habits that continue past the age most children outgrow them

If you notice any of these signs in your child's mouth, it's worth scheduling an orthodontic evaluation with a local orthodontist rather than waiting for the standard age 7 checkup.

Does Emotional Readiness Matter Too?

Dental development isn't the only consideration. A child's maturity plays a real role in how successfully they manage orthodontic treatment. Wearing braces requires consistent good oral hygiene, care around what they eat, and the discipline to keep up with appointments and, in some cases, removable appliances. Many parents find that involving their child in the decision — explaining why treatment matters for their oral health and their smile — helps kids feel more ready to take on the responsibility that comes with braces.

Treatment Options for Children

Once a child is ready for active treatment, there are several orthodontic treatment options to consider, depending on the specific orthodontic issues involved and family preference:

  • Traditional braces use metal brackets and wires to gradually move teeth into correct position and remain the most common and versatile option for correcting significant crowding or bite issues in children
  • Ceramic braces work the same way as traditional metal braces but use tooth-colored brackets that are less noticeable
  • Partial braces may be used for more limited corrections rather than a full set of brackets
  • Clear aligner options, such as Invisalign, may be appropriate for older children and teens with milder orthodontic problems

Your child's orthodontist will recommend the treatment option best suited to their specific bite issues, jaw growth pattern, and lifestyle.

Why Early Care Matters for Long-Term Oral Health

Early orthodontic treatment isn't just about achieving a healthy smile faster — it's about supporting a child's oral health for the long term. Crowded or misaligned teeth are harder to clean properly, which raises the risk of tooth decay and gum disease. Correcting bite issues and jaw growth patterns early can also mean a shorter, simpler treatment time later, since the orthodontist is working with a jaw that has already been guided into a better foundation.

Across the country, roughly four million people currently wear braces, and a growing number of them are children who received an early orthodontic evaluation that caught a developing issue before it became a bigger orthodontic problem.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. What is the best age to get braces for a child?

There's no single best age for every child, but most orthodontists recommend a first evaluation by age 7, with active treatment using full braces typically starting between ages 9 and 14 once most permanent teeth have erupted.

2. Is 7 years old too early for braces?

Age 7 is not too early for an orthodontic evaluation, though it's often too early for full braces. At this age, an orthodontist is usually monitoring jaw growth and looking for early signs of orthodontic problems rather than starting comprehensive treatment.

3. Does my 8-year-old really need an expander?

Not every 8-year-old needs an expander. Expanders are typically recommended during Phase One treatment for children with narrow jaws or significant crowding that could affect how permanent teeth come in. An orthodontic exam can determine whether your child's jaw growth would benefit from one.

4. Why would a 4-year-old go to an orthodontist?

While the standard first evaluation is recommended by age 7, some orthodontists may see younger children if a parent or pediatric dentist notices early signs of significant bite issues, mouth breathing, or unusual jaw development that could benefit from earlier monitoring.

5. What is the best age for a kid to get braces?

Most children begin active treatment with traditional braces between ages 10 and 14, once most permanent teeth have erupted and the bite is more predictable.

6. Is it okay for a 10-year-old to get braces?

Yes. Age 10 falls within the common age range many orthodontists consider optimal for starting comprehensive orthodontic treatment, especially once most permanent teeth are in place.

7. Should my 7-year-old get braces?

Most 7-year-olds are not ready for full braces since many permanent teeth haven't erupted yet. Age 7 is instead the recommended age for a first orthodontic evaluation to monitor development and catch early orthodontic issues.

8. What age are braces free?

Braces are not free at any age through orthodontic offices, though some dental insurance plans or public health programs may help cover costs for children under certain age or income requirements. Coverage varies widely across dental insurance plans, so it's worth checking your specific policy.

9. Is early orthodontic treatment necessary?

Early orthodontic treatment isn't necessary for every child, but for those with significant crowding, jaw growth issues, or functional problems like difficulty chewing, it can prevent more serious orthodontic issues later and may reduce the need for extractions or more extensive treatment down the road.

10. What is a Sunday bite in orthodontics?

This isn't a standard clinical term used in mainstream orthodontics. If you've encountered this phrase, it's best to ask your child's orthodontist directly, as it may refer to a specific appliance or technique used by an individual practice.
