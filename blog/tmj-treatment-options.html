TMJ Treatment

TMJ Treatment Options: From Night Guards and Botox to Orthodontic Care

Dante Gonzales Orthodontics August 1, 2026 18 min read

On this page

Temporomandibular joint TMJ disorders affect tens of millions of people and produce some of the most disruptive and difficult-to-diagnose pain conditions in dental medicine. The jaw joint and surrounding jaw muscles are involved in virtually every movement of daily life, from eating and talking to yawning and swallowing, which means that TMJ pain affects function in ways most other joint disorders simply do not. The good news is that the majority of temporomandibular disorders respond well to treatment, and most patients can achieve significant pain relief and restored jaw movement without ever needing surgery. Understanding the full range of TMJ treatment options, from conservative self care to minimally invasive procedures and orthodontic treatment, is the foundation of making an informed decision about your care.

What Are TMJ Disorders?

TMJ disorders, also called temporomandibular disorders or TMD, are a group of conditions that cause pain and dysfunction in the temporomandibular joint and the jaw muscles that control jaw movement. The temporomandibular joint TMJ connects the lower jaw to the temporal bone of the skull and is one of the most constantly used joints in the human body. When this joint or the surrounding tissues are affected by inflammation, misalignment, muscle tension, or structural damage, the result is a range of TMJ symptoms that can include jaw pain, facial pain, ear pain, persistent headaches, difficulty chewing, and locking or restricted jaw movement.

The etiology of TMJ disorders is multifactorial, including biological, environmental, social, emotional, and cognitive triggers. Common causes of temporomandibular disorders include teeth grinding, teeth clenching, jaw clenching, bite misalignment, arthritis, jaw injury, nail biting, gum chewing, and psychological stress. Factors consistently associated with TMJ disorders include other pain conditions such as fibromyalgia, autoimmune disorders, sleep apnea, and psychiatric conditions including depression and anxiety. Accurate diagnosis is the critical first step because TMD symptoms overlap significantly with other orofacial pain and pain conditions, and treating the wrong root cause produces no relief.

Common Symptoms of TMJ Disorders

Before exploring treatment options, understanding what TMJ disorders feel like helps patients recognize when their symptoms warrant professional evaluation. Common symptoms of temporomandibular joint dysfunction include:

  • Jaw pain and jaw discomfort that is worst in the morning or after long periods of jaw use
  • Facial pain and orofacial pain that radiates from the jaw joint into the cheeks, temples, and ear
  • Ear pain or a sensation of fullness in the ear without any identifiable ear condition
  • Persistent headaches and tension headaches originating from jaw muscle tension
  • Clicking, popping, or grating sounds during jaw movement when opening or closing the mouth
  • Restricted mouth opening or locking of the jaw in an open or closed position
  • Neck muscles pain and shoulder pain from the compensatory strain generated by ongoing jaw dysfunction
  • Misaligned teeth or a change in how the upper and lower teeth come together

TMD symptoms can vary considerably in severity. Mild discomfort that comes and goes with stress or overuse is very different from severe pain that is constant, limits mouth opening, and interferes with eating and sleeping. Understanding the severity and pattern of your specific TMJ symptoms helps your healthcare provider determine which treatment tier is most appropriate and how urgently treatment is needed.

TMJ Treatment Options: A Complete Overview

Treatment options for temporomandibular joint TMJ disorders are generally organized into three tiers: non-invasive conservative treatments, minimally invasive procedures, and surgical interventions. The TMJ Association and clinical guidelines consistently recommend starting with the most conservative, reversible treatments before advancing to more invasive options. Tier 1: Conservative and Non-Invasive TMJ Treatments

The vast majority of TMJ disorders respond to conservative treatments that do not involve surgery or permanent alteration of the jaw joint or teeth. Managing TMJ symptoms typically begins with these non-invasive approaches, which are effective for most patients with mild to moderate temporomandibular disorders. Non-invasive and conservative treatments are the first line of defense for TMJ disorders, as recommended by the National Institutes of Health.

Self-Care and Lifestyle Modifications

Simple daily adjustments can significantly reduce strain on the jaw joint and masticatory muscles for patients with mild or periodic TMJ symptoms. Eating soft foods and avoiding hard foods, raw vegetables, and chewy items reduces the mechanical load on the jaw joint during active flare-ups. Avoiding gum chewing and nail biting eliminates overuse habits that cause jaw muscle spasm. Applying moist heat to relax tight jaw muscles or an ice pack to reduce acute pain and swelling are effective self care strategies that ease pain and reduce discomfort between professional treatments.

Good posture throughout the day, particularly avoiding forward head position at a desk or during device use, reduces the strain on the neck muscles and jaw joint that drives tension headaches and persistent TMJ pain. Resting the jaw with teeth slightly apart and lips together, rather than clenching the teeth together, is the correct resting jaw posture that reduces unnecessary jaw muscle tension throughout the day.

Oral Appliances: Night Guards and Occlusal Splints

Oral appliances are among the most widely used and clinically supported non-invasive treatments for temporomandibular disorders. Custom-fitted night guards and occlusal splints prevent teeth grinding and teeth clenching during sleep, reducing the unnatural forces placed on the temporomandibular joint and jaw muscles overnight. These medical devices keep the jaw joint in a neutral, decompressed position during sleep, reducing jaw muscle tension, jaw pain, and the morning symptoms that define many patients' daily TMJ experience.

Intraoral appliances such as night guards and splints are commonly used to alleviate TMD symptoms, and while the evidence on their long-term effectiveness varies, most patients experience meaningful reduction in jaw pain and headache frequency with consistent nightly use. Oral appliances are most effective when combined with other conservative treatments addressing the behavioral and muscular components of TMJ dysfunction simultaneously.

Medications for TMJ Pain Relief

Medication is generally used short-term to manage acute pain and inflammation in patients with TMJ disorders rather than as a long-term standalone solution. The most commonly used medications for TMJ pain relief include:

  • Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs): Over-the-counter nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen are the first-line agents for the initial treatment of acute pain associated with TMJ disorders, typically prescribed or recommended for 10 to 14 days to reduce joint swelling and relieve pain
  • Muscle relaxants: Prescribed alongside NSAIDs when there is a muscular component to the TMJ disorder, muscle relaxants help alleviate jaw muscle spasm, ongoing pain, and jaw discomfort
  • Over-the-counter medications: Over the counter medications including acetaminophen provide mild discomfort relief for patients with less severe TMJ pain conditions who do not require prescription-strength anti-inflammatory treatment
  • Low-dose antidepressants: Tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline are used for managing chronic TMJ pain, particularly when other treatments have not been effective, by interrupting the central pain sensitization that drives persistent symptoms
  • Corticosteroid injections: Injected directly into the jaw joint to relieve acute inflammation and provide temporary pain relief during severe TMJ flare-ups

Physical Therapy for TMJ Disorders

Physical therapy is one of the most effective non-invasive treatment options for TMJ disorders with a significant muscular component. A physical therapist designs a personalized program of jaw exercises, manual therapy, and postural correction that addresses the specific jaw muscle tension and jaw movement restrictions driving the patient's TMJ symptoms. Physical therapy can improve joint mobility, relieve pain, and restore normal jaw movement in patients whose temporomandibular joint dysfunction is driven primarily by muscular imbalance and poor posture patterns.

Specific jaw exercises used in physical therapy for TMJ disorders include:

  • Relaxed jaw exercise: Gently opening and closing the mouth while keeping teeth apart and tongue on the roof of the mouth to reduce jaw muscle tension
  • Goldfish exercises (partial opening): Partially opening and closing the mouth to increase range of motion flexibility and decrease TMJ stiffness
  • Chin tucks: Tucking the chin toward the chest to improve posture and relieve tension in the neck muscles and jaw
  • Resisted mouth opening: Placing resistance under the chin while opening the mouth to strengthen jaw muscles and improve jaw joint stability
  • Side-to-side jaw movement: Stretching the muscles that support the temporomandibular joint to improve mobility and reduce jaw discomfort

Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) and acupuncture are complementary treatments used alongside physical therapy that have been studied for their potential benefits in managing TMD symptoms and reducing orofacial pain. Relaxation techniques, cognitive behavioral therapy, and biofeedback are self-management strategies that address the psychological causes of TMJ disorders including stress, anxiety, and subconscious jaw clenching.

Tier 2: Minimally Invasive TMJ Treatments

When conservative treatments do not adequately relieve TMJ pain or persistent symptoms return despite consistent self care and oral appliance use, minimally invasive procedures are the next appropriate step. These treatments address more specific structural or muscular root causes of TMJ disorders without the risks and recovery associated with open joint surgery.

Botox Injections for TMJ

Injecting botulinum toxin type A into the masseter and temporalis jaw muscles is one of the most effective minimally invasive treatments for TMJ disorders driven by chronic jaw clenching, teeth grinding, and jaw muscle tension. Botox works by temporarily blocking nerve signals to the overactive jaw muscles, reducing muscle contraction force and relieving the TMJ pain, headaches, and jaw discomfort that muscular overactivity generates. The effects of Botox for TMJ typically last 3 to 4 months, after which repeat treatments maintain ongoing pain relief.

Botox injections are particularly valuable for patients with significant bruxism, enlarged masseter muscles, and treatment-resistant jaw muscle tension that has not responded to oral appliances or physical therapy alone. Botulinum toxin type A has been studied extensively for its potential to relieve TMJ pain by relaxing jaw muscles, and while it is classified as an off-label treatment by the FDA specifically for TMJ disorders, it is widely used and clinically supported as part of a comprehensive TMJ treatment plan.

Trigger Point Injections

Trigger point injections deliver corticosteroids or local anesthetic directly into specific painful points within the jaw muscles and neck muscles that are generating referred TMJ pain. These injections relax the muscle fibers at the trigger point, interrupt the pain cycle, and provide direct pain relief in cases where myofascial pain is a primary contributor to the patient's TMJ symptoms. Trigger point injections are typically used alongside physical therapy and other conservative treatments rather than as a standalone TMJ treatment option.

Arthrocentesis

Arthrocentesis is a minimally invasive procedure that involves using a needle to inject liquid into the temporomandibular joint to flush out inflammatory byproducts, remove scar-like tissue, and reduce inflammation within the jaw joint. It is typically performed under local anesthesia as an in-office procedure and provides temporary pain relief and improved mouth opening for patients with moderate structural TMJ involvement that has not responded to nonsurgical treatments. Arthrocentesis is considerably less invasive than arthroscopy or open joint surgery and is often the first procedural intervention considered when conservative treatments are insufficient.

Tier 3: Surgical TMJ Treatments

Surgical options for TMJ disorders are a last resort, reserved for patients with severe pain, significant structural joint degradation, and confirmed failure of conservative and minimally invasive treatments. Invasive options are considered only for severe, chronic cases that do not respond to conservative management, as recommended by the TMJ Association and clinical guidelines. The decision to involve surgery requires thorough evaluation including magnetic resonance imaging to assess the internal structures of the jaw joint before any surgical plan is developed.

Arthroscopy

Arthroscopy allows a surgeon to insert a tiny video camera into the jaw joint through a small incision to diagnose and treat structural issues directly, such as removing adhesions, repositioning the displaced articular disc, or flushing the joint. This procedure has been shown to moderately improve pain and jaw function for patients with internal derangement that has not responded to other treatments. Arthroscopy is significantly less invasive than open joint surgery, carries fewer risks, and requires a shorter recovery period.

Open Joint Surgery

Open joint surgery is the most invasive TMJ treatment option and is reserved for severe structural joint degradation that cannot be addressed through arthroscopy or other procedures. Surgical treatments for TMJ disorders include arthroscopy, open joint surgery, and orthognathic surgery to realign jaws. In the most severe cases of joint destruction, total joint replacement using TMJ implants may be considered, though concerns about long-term safety and efficacy mean this option is approached with significant caution and used only when all other options have been exhausted. Any decision to involve surgery for TMJ disorders should involve consultation with an oral and maxillofacial surgeon alongside the patient's existing dental and medical healthcare providers.

The Role of Orthodontic Treatment in TMJ Management

For patients whose temporomandibular joint pain and dysfunction has a bite alignment component, orthodontic treatment plays a clinically meaningful role in comprehensive TMJ management. Misaligned teeth create uneven distribution of bite forces across the jaw joint and jaw muscles, placing chronic, disproportionate stress on the temporomandibular joint that contributes to TMJ pain, jaw muscle tension, and progressive joint wear over time.

Correcting misaligned teeth through Invisalign or other orthodontic treatment eliminates this source of structural TMJ stress, reducing the forces on the jaw joint that drive ongoing pain and dysfunction. At Dante Gonzales Orthodontics, orthodontic treatment for TMJ-related bite misalignment is evaluated as part of a comprehensive assessment that considers the full pattern of the patient's TMJ symptoms, treatment history, and jaw anatomy. Orthodontic treatment is not a standalone solution for all forms of TMJ disorders, but for patients with a clear bite misalignment component, it is an important and lasting part of the overall treatment plan.

When Is TMJ Serious? Knowing When to Seek Help

Most mild or short-term TMJ issues respond well to conservative, reversible habits and self care. However, certain patterns of TMJ symptoms indicate a more serious condition that requires prompt professional evaluation:

  • Jaw locking that prevents full opening or closing of the mouth
  • Severe pain that does not respond to over-the-counter medications or self care
  • Persistent symptoms that worsen over weeks despite conservative management
  • Significant changes in bite alignment or the way the upper and lower teeth come together
  • TMJ pain accompanied by facial swelling, fever, or neurological symptoms

Early treatment is consistently associated with better outcomes in temporomandibular disorders. The longer significant TMJ pain and dysfunction goes unaddressed, the more complex the underlying joint and muscle changes become, and the less likely conservative treatments are to provide complete relief. If your TMJ symptoms are interfering with eating, sleeping, or daily function, a comprehensive evaluation by a TMJ specialist or orthodontist experienced in orofacial pain is the most important step you can take.

How to Make TMJ Go Away Faster: Key Principles

While there is no single permanent cure for all forms of TMJ disorders, most patients achieve significant and lasting pain relief by following these core principles of effective TMJ management:

  • Start with conservative treatments and give them sufficient time before moving to more invasive options
  • Address the root causes, not just the symptoms — whether that means stress reduction, bite correction, or physical therapy for jaw muscle tension
  • Be consistent with self care strategies including soft foods, jaw rest, good posture, and relaxation techniques every day
  • Wear oral appliances as directed every night without exception to protect the jaw joint from nighttime grinding and clenching
  • Communicate openly with your healthcare provider about what is working, what is not, and any changes in your TMJ symptoms
  • Seek accurate diagnosis early rather than waiting for symptoms to become severe before pursuing evaluation and treatment

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. What Is the Best Treatment for TMJ Disorder?

The best treatment for TMJ disorder depends on the type of temporomandibular disorder, the severity of TMJ symptoms, and the underlying root causes. For most patients, conservative non-invasive treatments including oral appliances, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physical therapy, soft foods, and relaxation techniques deliver effective pain relief and restored jaw movement. When conservative treatments are insufficient, minimally invasive options such as Botox injections or arthrocentesis are the next step. Surgery involving open joint procedures is a last resort reserved for severe cases with confirmed structural joint degradation.

2. Can Physical Therapy Fix TMJ?

Physical therapy can significantly improve TMJ disorders that are primarily driven by jaw muscle tension, muscular imbalance, and poor posture. Jaw exercises, manual therapy, and postural correction prescribed by a physical therapist help restore normal jaw movement, relieve pain, and address the neck muscles and surrounding tissues that contribute to TMJ pain. Physical therapy is most effective when combined with oral appliances and self care strategies, and it is particularly valuable for patients with myofascial TMJ pain. It is less effective as a standalone treatment for structural joint derangement or severe TMJ disorders with significant cartilage damage.

3. What Not to Do With TMJ Disorder?

Patients with TMJ disorders should avoid hard foods, gum chewing, nail biting, and wide jaw movements such as yawning broadly or taking very large bites, as these activities increase the mechanical load on the jaw joint and worsen TMJ symptoms. Avoid clenching the teeth together at rest, resting the chin on the hand, and poor posture habits that strain the neck muscles and jaw. Avoid delaying treatment for persistent symptoms, as early treatment consistently produces better outcomes than waiting for TMJ pain to become severe before seeking evaluation.

4. What Is the 3-Finger Test for TMJ?

The 3-finger test is a simple clinical screening tool for restricted jaw opening associated with TMJ disorders. To perform it, stack three fingers vertically and attempt to place them between your upper and lower teeth with your mouth open as wide as comfortably possible. If you cannot fit all three fingers without significant pain or resistance, restricted mouth opening is present and warrants professional evaluation by a TMJ specialist or healthcare provider. Normal mouth opening should comfortably accommodate three stacked fingers without pain.

5. Is TMJ a Lifelong Problem?

TMJ is not necessarily a lifelong problem. Many patients with mild to moderate temporomandibular disorders achieve complete or near-complete pain relief and restored jaw movement through consistent conservative treatment and do not experience ongoing significant TMJ symptoms. Patients with more severe structural joint degeneration or chronic pain sensitization may require ongoing management rather than a definitive cure, but effective treatment significantly reduces the impact of TMJ disorders on daily life even in complex cases. Early treatment and consistent management of the root causes produce the best long-term outcomes.

6. How Do You Calm a TMJ Flare-Up?

To calm a TMJ flare-up quickly, apply moist heat to the jaw and temples to relax tight jaw muscles, or use an ice pack to reduce acute pain and swelling in the jaw joint. Take over-the-counter nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen to reduce pain and inflammation. Rest the jaw by eating soft foods only, avoiding wide jaw movements, and keeping teeth slightly apart at rest. Practice relaxation techniques to reduce the stress-driven jaw clenching that typically worsens flare-ups. If the flare-up is severe or accompanied by locking of the jaw, seek evaluation from your healthcare provider or TMJ specialist promptly.
