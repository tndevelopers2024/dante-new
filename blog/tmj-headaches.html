TMJ Headaches: What Causes Them and How to Find Lasting Relief

Dante Gonzales Orthodontics August 1, 2026 10 min read

On this page

If you regularly experience headaches that no amount of pain medication fully resolves, your jaw may be the root cause. TMJ headaches, headaches caused or worsened by temporomandibular joint TMJ dysfunction, are among the most frequently misdiagnosed causes of chronic head pain. Many patients spend years treating the headaches alone without realizing the jaw joint and surrounding jaw muscles are driving the entire cycle. At Dante Gonzales Orthodontics, identifying the root cause of TMJ-related headaches is where lasting relief begins.

What Are TMJ Headaches?

TMJ headaches are headaches that originate from dysfunction in the temporomandibular joint, the jaw joint connecting the lower jaw to the skull, or from the jaw muscles and facial muscles that support it. When TMJ disorder affects the jaw joint through joint inflammation, joint misalignment, or muscle overload from teeth grinding and jaw clenching, the resulting tension, nerve irritation, and referred pain produce headaches that are virtually indistinguishable from tension headaches or migraines without a professional evaluation.

Headaches related to TMJ disorders arise from muscle tension, nerve irritation, or joint inflammation that radiates pain into the temples, forehead, or behind the eyes. TMJ disorder affects millions of patients whose chronic headaches are jaw-related symptoms that never resolve because the jaw joint is never treated. An accurate diagnosis makes a big difference between treating the symptom and solving the problem at the root cause.

What Does a TMJ Headache Feel Like?

TMJ headaches often feel like tension headaches, characterized by pressure, tightness, or a dull ache on one or both sides of the head. A TMJ headache usually starts around the temples and jaw, radiating to the neck, forehead, and shoulder. The pain is typically a persistent dull ache rather than the sharp, throbbing quality of migraine pain, though in patients prone to migraines, TMJ disorder can trigger full migraine attacks.

Symptoms That Accompany TMJ Headaches

Patients who experience jaw pain and TMJ headaches typically also report:

  • Tightness across the temples and jaw muscles that worsens through the day
  • Headaches that are worst in the morning after nighttime teeth grinding or jaw clenching
  • Pain that increases when chewing hard foods, opening the mouth wide, or yawning
  • Neck and shoulder pain alongside the head pain, as surrounding muscles overcompensate for jaw misalignment
  • Jaw clicking or popping sounds accompanying the TMJ-related headaches
  • Trouble chewing or a sense of jaw fatigue after meals

Can TMJ Problems Cause Migraines?

Yes, TMJ problems can trigger migraines. TMJ disorders can cause both headaches and migraines through increased central sensitization, which makes the brain more reactive to pain signals. The trigeminal nerve, responsible for facial sensation, is activated by both migraines and TMJ issues, leading to overlapping migraine symptoms and diagnostic difficulty. For patients already prone to migraines, chronic TMJ-related headaches lower the migraine threshold, increasing both migraine frequency and migraine pain intensity.

TMJ Headaches vs. Migraines: Key Differences

Because TMJ headaches and migraines share overlapping symptoms through shared trigeminal nerve pathways, distinguishing between them requires a comprehensive evaluation and medical history review. The table below outlines the key differences:

When TMJ treatment is applied to the jaw-related component, overall headache frequency and migraine pain intensity typically reduce, even in patients with a confirmed migraine diagnosis. Getting the right treatment for the right root cause makes a big difference in daily life and quality of sleep.

What Causes TMJ Headaches?

Teeth Grinding and Jaw Clenching

Bruxism, habitual teeth grinding and jaw clenching, is the most common driver of TMJ headaches. Clenching or grinding activates the jaw muscles with excessive force, generating muscle tension that radiates into the temples and head. Stress and anxiety provoke unconscious jaw clenching and tightening of facial muscles, contributing to TMJ-related headaches that are worst first thing in the morning or after a stressful day.

Joint Inflammation and Arthritis

Joint inflammation from degenerative conditions like arthritis or direct jaw trauma creates ongoing pain signals the nervous system interprets as headaches. When the jaw joint is inflamed, surrounding muscles tense protectively, adding muscle tension headaches on top of joint-driven pain. Arthritis affecting the temporomandibular joint is a frequently overlooked cause of chronic headaches, particularly in older patients.

Poor Posture and Joint Misalignment

Misalignment of the temporomandibular joint forces neck and shoulder muscles to overcompensate, leading to tension headaches that originate in the cervical spine and radiate upward. Poor posture, particularly forward head posture during extended screen time, changes the resting position of the lower jaw and increases mechanical load on the jaw joint and jaw muscles throughout the day, worsening TMJ disorder affects and headache frequency.

Sleep Apnea and Nighttime Grinding

Sleep apnea and TMJ disorder frequently co-exist. Nighttime airway obstruction triggers jaw repositioning and teeth grinding during sleep, producing severe TMJ headaches and jaw pain upon waking. Patients experiencing this pattern of morning headaches, jaw soreness, and neck tension should seek evaluation for both conditions, addressing sleep apnea and TMJ issues together is essential for breaking the cycle.

TMJ Headache Treatment Options

Managing TMJ headaches effectively requires addressing both the immediate symptoms and the underlying mechanical causes. The right treatment plan is built from a comprehensive evaluation and tailored to the specific jaw-related root cause identified by your healthcare provider or TMJ specialist.

Oral Appliances

Custom-fitted oral appliances, bite guards and splints, keep the jaw joint in the correct resting position and prevent clenching or grinding during sleep. These dental devices make a significant difference for patients whose TMJ headaches are driven by nighttime bruxism, reducing morning headaches and jaw pain within the first weeks of consistent nightly use.

Physical Therapy and Jaw Exercises

Physical therapy, including jaw exercises, manual therapy, and physical therapy exercises targeting the jaw and neck, strengthens and stretches the jaw muscles, reducing the muscle tension that generates TMJ-related headaches. A physical therapist designs specific jaw exercises to restore joint mobility and correct the poor posture patterns that contribute to ongoing tension headaches.

Botox and Muscle Relaxers

Muscle relaxers and botulinum toxin injections reduce TMJ headaches by alleviating muscle tightness and tension in the jaw muscles. Botox injected into the masseter and temporalis jaw muscles reduces clenching intensity that drives headaches, with lasting relief typically maintained for three to six months per treatment. Stress management techniques, mindfulness, biofeedback, and relaxation exercises, address the psychological root cause of jaw clenching that contributes to both TMJ headaches and migraine pain.

Self-Care for TMJ Headaches

For day-to-day self care relief of TMJ headaches between treatment appointments:

  • Apply moist heat to the jaw and temples to relax tight jaw muscles and ease headache tension
  • Avoid hard foods and chewy foods that increase load on the jaw joint during active flare-ups
  • Maintain good posture throughout the day, particularly at a desk, to reduce neck and jaw muscle strain
  • Keep teeth slightly apart at rest rather than clenching, rest the jaw with lips together, teeth apart
  • Limit wide jaw movements such as yawning broadly or taking oversized bites

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. What Do TMJ Headaches Feel Like?

TMJ headaches feel like tension headaches, a dull ache, pressure, or tightness on one or both sides of the head that typically starts around the temples and jaw and radiates toward the neck and forehead. They are usually worst in the morning after nighttime teeth grinding or jaw clenching and worsen with jaw use such as chewing hard foods or opening the mouth wide. Unlike primary migraines, TMJ headaches are consistently linked to jaw-related symptoms including jaw pain, clicking, and trouble chewing.

2. Can TMJ Problems Cause Migraines?

Yes, TMJ disorder can trigger and worsen migraines. The trigeminal nerve, which carries sensation across the face and jaw, is activated by both TMJ issues and migraines, creating overlapping migraine symptoms that make accurate diagnosis challenging without a professional evaluation. TMJ disorders also increase central sensitization, lowering the migraine threshold and increasing both migraine frequency and migraine pain intensity. Effective TMJ treatment frequently reduces overall headache and migraine frequency in patients with co-existing TMJ and migraine conditions.

3. How Do You Get Rid of a TMJ Headache?

Getting rid of TMJ headaches requires treating the jaw-related root cause rather than managing headache pain alone. Effective TMJ treatment options include custom bite guards to prevent nighttime grinding and clenching, Botox or muscle relaxers to reduce jaw muscle tension, physical therapy exercises to restore jaw joint mobility and correct poor posture, and stress management techniques to reduce unconscious jaw clenching. Self care strategies, applying moist heat, avoiding hard foods, and maintaining good posture, provide additional day-to-day relief between professional treatment appointments.

4. What Are the Odd Symptoms of TMJ Disorder?

Beyond jaw pain and headaches, TMJ disorder affects patients in unexpected ways. Odd symptoms of TMJ disorders include ringing in the ears (tinnitus), a sensation of fullness or blockage in the ears, dizziness, unexplained tooth sensitivity, facial numbness or tingling, and difficulty fully opening or closing the mouth. Sleep apnea and TMJ disorder frequently co-exist, with nighttime airway obstruction triggering jaw clenching that generates morning headaches and neck tension. These jaw-related symptoms are often misattributed to other conditions until a comprehensive TMJ evaluation identifies the jaw joint as the source.

5. What Is the 3-Finger Test for TMJ?

The 3-finger test is a simple screening tool for TMJ disorder and restricted jaw opening. Stack three fingers vertically, index, middle, and ring, and attempt to place them between your upper and lower teeth with your mouth open as wide as comfortably possible. If you cannot fit all three fingers without significant pain or resistance, restricted jaw movement is present and a professional evaluation by a TMJ specialist or healthcare provider is recommended to assess for TMJ disorder or joint dysfunction.
